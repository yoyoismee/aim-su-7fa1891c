    Ask the brick · before the datasheet

    We built with wood for ten thousand years before anyone wrote the spec.

    The hazard rows got written afterwards. Usually by whoever got hurt.

    This material is four years old.

    Also: teak and pine are both "wood." Two things are called "AI." We'll need two datasheets.

    1830s
    Balloon framing. Fast, cheap, built Chicago. Every wall cavity a chimney. Fire-stopping only became code a century later.
    1965–73
    Aluminium wiring. Copper got expensive, so two million American homes were wired in aluminium. The joints loosen and arc. Fifty-five times the fire hazard. The standard changed in 1972.
    1970s–2003
    Arsenic-treated timber. Rot-proof decking and playgrounds for thirty years. Phased out when the arsenic turned out to leach into children's hands.
    today
    Treated utility poles. Dirt and rain on an insulator let current leak into the wood until it smoulders. The preservative that stops rot helps it burn.
    Each of these worked. Each shipped. The hazard row came later. Nobody lied; they just hadn't asked the question yet. The aluminium one is the "American electrical disaster" half-remembered in most tellings. Your own story goes here, if you want it: a material you found, packed, shipped, and only then understood.     Intelligence

    1950

    I propose to consider the question, "Can machines think?"Alan Turing · Computing Machinery and Intelligence

    Then he gave up on the question and swapped in a game. If you can't tell it from a person in conversation, it counts.

    1966

    ELIZA matched patterns and turned your sentence back into a question. Its creator's secretary asked him to leave the room so she could talk to it in private. He had built it to show how shallow the trick was. It did the opposite.

    If it acts like us, we call it intelligent. We're a modest species.

    Behavior is the test. Is that enough?

    Turing and anthropomorphism in one breath. The test says "acts like a person," and we are built to see persons everywhere: Peter Rabbit, your car, the chat window. So the test is easy to pass and says as much about the judge as the machine. ELIZA, told slowly: 1966, MIT, Joseph Weizenbaum. Named after Eliza Doolittle, who passes by imitation. The therapist script was a dodge: a Rogerian therapist may know nothing and ask everything, so the emptiness went unnoticed. "I feel tired" → "Why do you feel tired?" His secretary watched him build it for months, knew exactly what it was, and after a few exchanges asked him to leave the room. He wrote in 1976 that he hadn't realised "extremely short exposures to a relatively simple computer program could induce powerful delusional thinking in quite normal people." Psychiatrists proposed using it on patients; he spent the rest of his life arguing some things shouldn't be automated even if they can be. Then and now: Her, 2013. Replika and Character.AI, millions of daily users. August 2025: OpenAI retires GPT-4o and the outcry from people attached to its voice brings it back within a day. Sixty years, same request: don't leave the room. For this room: the trick worked because listening, even fake listening, is rare. The model is the best fake listener ever built. Real listening is the job, and it comes back as "discover the actual context." Let them say "not enough" to Turing.

    Guess. Measure the error. Nudge.

    The client says "coral." You can't see the sample. You can only ask: how far off am I? Turn the knobs until the number stops shrinking.

    That number is all the machine ever gets. It does the same loop, except nobody told it what "red" means, let alone "coral." Working out which knob to turn, and which way, is called backpropagation.

    Three knobs here.

    Let the room shout "more red!" and press the buttons for them. Watch the error. When they get stuck, reveal the sample. Then press "Let the machine do it" and let them watch the error fall in a dozen steps. They had a head start: they know what red is and roughly what coral is. The machine starts from nothing but the number. Colour names use common web values, so a designer may disagree with "coral", which is itself a nice aside.

    How it works · who's the judge?

    After reading, it gets a coach.

    Judge one: a human. Paid per click, shown two answers, picks the nicer one. Thousands of them. The model is pushed toward whatever wins.

    Judge two: an answer key. Math, code, puzzles with a checkable result. Right gets a point. That's where the "reasoning" models came from.

    It learns to please the judge. Neither judge is your client.

    Amazon named its labelling marketplace Mechanical Turk, after the chess machine with the man inside. The man is still inside.

    RLHF and RLVR, don't say the acronyms unless asked. Human judge: averaged taste, tired, paid per click. Answer key judge: only works where an answer key exists. Design has neither. This is why it's agreeable and why it's confident.

    Chain of thought. The reasoning models are this plus the answer-key judge. It genuinely helps on anything with steps. It does not add a site visit.

    Tools aren't magic.

    The model can't browse, measure, or open your SketchUp file. It writes a line of text asking for it. A normal program does the thing and pastes the answer back into the conversation. The model reads it and carries on guessing words.

    "Agent" means this loop runs many times without you watching.

    A text box that can ask for things. That's the whole trick.

    Demystify. Everything "agentic" is this loop. Which means every error in the loop is still a next-word guess somewhere.

    How it works · text, so far

    More capability. Same material.

    Each layer makes it more useful, and it looks more powerful every year. Look at the middle. The source hasn't changed. The judge hasn't changed. The guess hasn't changed.

    A better-dressed guess is still a guess.

    Hold that. Now pictures.

    Closes the text half. Walk the boxes from outside in: agents are tools in a loop, tools are text requests, thinking is more guessing in smaller pieces, the coach only reshapes what reading produced. Everything we add is wrapped around the same core, and every row of the spec sheet comes from that core. Then pivot: pictures went through the same story, in a different order.

    How it works · the picture · first, copying

    pictures · 2014 to 2019

    Copying was solved first.

    The early models learned what bedrooms look like from a pile of bedrooms, and handed you another plausible member of the pile. Realistic enough by 2019. And you had no say in it. No words, no sketch, nothing to hold. Another one like those, please.

    Realism was never the hard part. Control was.

    Everything since is the story of adding handles.

    Reframe the timeline from the start of the talk: those grids got realistic fast, and that is the least interesting fact about them. The GAN era had one input, the dataset. The limiting factor was never how good the picture looked; it was what the model could understand, what you could tell it, what you could steer. The next four pages are the handles arriving one by one: words, lines, references, and the dial of how much is yours.

    Double Diamond
    
  
    
    
  
    
  
    
    
    The shaded halves open up. The open halves close down. It can help you open up. You still do the work. And never ask it to close down for you.
    
    
    It will, happily. That's the problem. Converging is your job.
    
  
    
  
    Overview, then one page per phase. The sharp rule: it's a fine assistant for expanding, and you're still the one doing the expanding. Converging, picking, deciding, is the design, and the model will do it for you the moment you ask, which is exactly why you shouldn't. "Pick the best one" is the most dangerous prompt in the course. Each phase: what it does, what's yours, the trap. "Your job" is on the page, the adjective is yours.
    

    


    
  
    Double Diamond · discover
    
  
    
    
    Discover the internet.
    
    
    Precedent, references, materials, a summary of the building code. What's been done. It's great at this.
    
    
    Trap: it's the average internet, already feed-filtered. And it invents sources. Check anything you'll cite.
    
  
    
  
    
    
    Discover the actual context.
    
    
    Site, light, client, body, budget, culture, story. It wasn't there. Your job.
    
    
    Trap: the first kind felt like enough, so you skip this one. This one is what makes it design instead of decoration.
    
  
    

    


    
  
    Double Diamond · define
    
  
    
    
    It can write fifty briefs in a minute.
    
    
    Picking one is a point of view. Shaping it is design. Your job.
    
    
    Trap: a brief that fits everyone fits no one. This is where "built for everyone" meets your project head on.
    
  
    

    


    
  
    Double Diamond · develop
    
  
    
    
    Developing is navigating toward the one.
    
    
    Not generating as many as possible. A hundred variations isn't a hundred steps forward. It's one step and a pile. It makes each step cheap. Which direction to step is the design. Your job.
    
    
    Trap one: it varies around the mean, it doesn't change the frame. Trap two: a polished render looks finished, so you stop thinking too early. The hard decisions, dimensions, structure, ergonomics, live in CAD and BIM, where this material is weakest.
    
  
    
  
    Steer, don't spray.
    

    


    
  
    Double Diamond · deliver
    
  
    
    
    Renders, fly-throughs, the client email. Yes, please.
    
    
    Nobody minds the help. Nobody will ask whether the fly-through was hand-animated. But the quality of what leaves your hands is your job. If it makes bad work and your name is on it, that's on you.
    
    
    The tool doesn't sign the drawing.
    
    
    Trap: communication isn't design. A beautiful walk through an unbuildable room. Construction documents need exact, so back to CAD.
    
  
    
  
    Callback to the meat proxy: the proxy forwards output without owning it. The designer owns it.

    Where it's going · 1 of 2
    
  
    
    
  
    
  
    
    
    World models.
    
    
    Next word. Next frame. Next state. The newest models are trained to predict what happens when something acts in a space: open the door, push the chair, move the sun to four o'clock. Google's Genie makes a playable world from one picture. The physics still wobbles.
    
    
    The thing it never had: a room that pushes back.
    
    
    Early. The demos are stunning, the consistency isn't. This is the row of the datasheet to watch.
    
  
    
  
    Same loop, one more step out: instead of the next word, the next moment, conditioned on an action. Genie 3 (DeepMind, August 2025) runs a playable world in real time from a prompt; Sora was pitched as a "world simulator" in 2024; LeCun has argued for years that a world model is the missing piece, not more text. Be honest about status: objects still vanish, geometry drifts, nothing is measured. For the room: this is the first version of the material that could, one day, know that the stairs don't reach.
    

    


    
  
    Where it's going · 2 of 2
    
  
    
    
    
  
    
  
    
    
    Physical AI.
    
    
    The same loop, in a body. Cameras in, motor commands out. Trained on video of people doing things, then on months of falling over in simulation. The humanoids are going to factories first, and they still fall over a lot.
    
    
    This is the one that finally gets burned.
    
    
    Back to the stove. It had learned "hot" from ten million sentences and never touched anything. These will touch things. Then we find out whether that was the missing piece.
    
    
    And notice where the frontier is heading: bodies in rooms. That's your subject. You know things about it that aren't on the internet.
    
  
    
  
    "Physical AI" is NVIDIA's phrase (Jensen Huang, CES 2025). Robot foundation models: Google's RT-2, Physical Intelligence's π0, Figure, Tesla Optimus, Unitree's cheap humanoids. Brooks' line from the intelligence chapter: intelligence needs a body in a world, and the labs have come round to it. Honest status: demos are teleoperated more often than the videos admit; in-home use is years out. For the room: the knowledge of how bodies move through rooms, where people bump, how light and height and threshold feel, is not in any dataset. They have it.
